# Nobody's in Charge of Pizza: What Prices Are Really Telling You

> **Quick answer:** Prices aren't arbitrary numbers set to annoy you —
> they're the only mechanism that reliably tells millions of strangers
> how scarce something is right now, without anyone coordinating it
> centrally. When a price rises, it's telling you demand has outpaced
> supply; when it falls, the opposite. Reading prices as information,
> rather than reacting to them as an injustice, is one of the most
> useful financial skills you can build.

There's a well-known exchange between Margaret Thatcher and Mikhail Gorbachev. He asked how Britain made sure people got fed. Her answer: "I don't. Prices do." No single person or office decides how much bread reaches London each morning, or where the fresh fish from Norway should go. Prices do that job, continuously, without anyone being in charge.

## Who decides how much pizza a country needs?

Imagine being put in charge of every pizza shop in a country. Your job: make sure everyone who wants pizza can get it, today, in every city and town, with the right toppings, without running out or making too much. You'd need to know demand in every location, for every day, and coordinate cheese, tomatoes, dough, ovens, and delivery accordingly — a task that changes every single day.

No person, committee, or algorithm has ever solved this problem by planning it directly. But it gets solved anyway, every day, in remote towns and huge cities alike — because prices carry the information instead. When pizza is scarce somewhere, prices there rise, and suppliers rush in to capture that opportunity. When there's a glut, prices drop and suppliers pull back. The system self-corrects constantly, without central coordination.

## Why does a rising price feel unfair — and what should you actually do about it?

A price increase often feels like an injustice, especially for things you need. But a price isn't the cause of scarcity — it's the *messenger*. Something being scarce is the real problem; the price is just telling you so, honestly and immediately, before you'd otherwise find out the hard way (an empty shelf).

That reframe changes what the useful response is:

- **A rising price on something you need regularly** (energy, groceries, a rate on debt) is a signal to look for substitutes or reduce dependence, not just to feel aggrieved by the number.
- **A rising price on something you're selling** (your time, a skill, a property) is a signal that demand has increased, and you may be underpricing.
- **A falling price** is a signal of abundance or falling demand — useful information for timing a purchase, or for a business deciding whether to expand a product line.

## How does this apply to your own budget?

Your budget is full of prices sending you information every month — interest rates, utility bills, subscription costs, the yield on your savings. Treating each one as data rather than as background noise is the same skill Thatcher was describing, just applied at household scale. When a bill's price rises faster than the rest, that's the market telling you something changed — worth investigating rather than just absorbing.
