# Zero-Based Budgeting: How to Give Every Pound a Job (UK Guide)

> **Quick answer:** Zero-based budgeting means you plan every pound of your
> take-home pay before the month starts, until income minus everything you've
> planned to spend, save and repay equals exactly £0. "Zero" doesn't mean an
> empty account. It means no pound is left without a job. Savings, sinking
> funds and extra debt payments are jobs too.

Most budgets fail quietly. You set a figure for groceries and a figure for bills, and whatever's left over is "spare". Spare money doesn't stay spare for long. It drifts into takeaways, small online orders and a few things you don't remember buying, and by the 25th you're wondering where it went.

Zero-based budgeting closes that gap by giving the leftover money a job too. It's an old idea. Our grandparents did it with envelopes and a biscuit tin. It works just as well with a bank app.

## What is zero-based budgeting?

Zero-based budgeting is a way of planning your money where your income and your planned outgoings match exactly. You start from your take-home pay and keep assigning amounts to categories until nothing is unassigned:

**Take-home pay − (bills + spending + savings + debt repayments) = £0**

Each category is a "job" for a set number of pounds. When a pound has a job, you know what it's for before you spend it, and it's much harder to spend it by accident.

You might also see "zero-based budgeting" used for businesses. In accounting, it means a company justifying every cost from scratch each year instead of rolling last year's budget forward. The idea is related, because nothing is assumed and everything is justified, but this guide is about personal and household budgeting.

## How is it different from the 50-30-20 rule?

The [50-30-20 rule](/blog/what-the-50-30-20-rule-looks-like/) splits your income into three percentages: needs, wants and savings. It's a good starting point, but it's broad. It tells you roughly how much to spend on "wants". It doesn't tell you whether that money is for a weekend away or for this month's Christmas fund.

Zero-based budgeting goes one level deeper:

- **50-30-20** → three big buckets, set as percentages of income
- **Zero-based** → as many categories as your life needs, each set in pounds

You can use both. Many people start with 50-30-20 to check their overall balance, then switch to zero-based once they want to know exactly where each pound goes.

## How do you make a zero-based budget?

1. **Start with what actually lands in your account.** Use your take-home pay after tax, National Insurance, pension contributions and student loan, not your salary.
2. **List your fixed bills.** Rent or mortgage, council tax, energy, water, phone and broadband, insurance.
3. **Add your regular spending.** Groceries, transport, toiletries, prescriptions.
4. **Add the expenses you know are coming.** The car insurance renewal, Christmas, birthdays, the boiler service. Divide each yearly cost by 12 and save that every month into a [sinking fund](/blog/sinking-funds-envelope-method/).
5. **Give the future a job.** Emergency fund, extra debt payments, saving or investing.
6. **Assign whatever's left.** Fun money, eating out, a small buffer. Keep going until the total equals your take-home pay.

If you finish with money left over, it hasn't got a job yet. Give it one, usually savings or debt. If you finish with less than £0, something has to come down before the month starts, not after.

## Worked Example: a £2,100 a month zero-based budget

Here is one month for Sam, a single renter outside London. These numbers are illustrative, not a recommendation for your situation.

- Take-home pay: £2,100 a month
- Rent: £750
- Council tax: £120
- Energy: £95
- Water: £35
- Phone and broadband: £45
- Contents insurance: £12
- Groceries: £280
- Transport: £110
- Subscriptions: £25
- Clothing: £35
- Sinking fund, car insurance (£480 a year): £40
- Sinking fund, Christmas (£600 a year): £50
- Gifts and birthdays: £20
- Emergency fund: £150
- Extra credit card payment: £100
- Fun money: £120
- Eating out: £60
- Buffer: £53

→ Total assigned: £2,100 · Left without a job: £0

The £53 buffer isn't a cheat. It's a job: "small things I didn't plan for." If it isn't used, it rolls into the emergency fund at month end.

## What happens when you overspend a category?

You will, especially in the first two or three months. The rule is simple: **money moves between jobs, it doesn't appear from nowhere.**

If Sam spends £310 on groceries instead of £280, the extra £30 has to come from another category this month, perhaps fun money or eating out. The total still equals £0. What you don't do is quietly go overdrawn and promise to "sort it out next month". That is how one bad month turns into a habit.

Overspending the same category every month isn't a failure. It's information. Raise that category at the monthly review and lower another one.

## Is zero-based budgeting good for beginners?

Yes, if you keep it small at the start. The most common beginner mistake is creating 40 categories in week one and giving up in week three. Begin with 10 to 15 categories, use the same ones for three months, and only then split or merge them.

It also helps to budget on the day you're paid, not on the 1st of the month. Your budget month should start when the money arrives.

Zero-based budgeting works especially well if:

- your income is tight and every pound matters
- you keep getting caught out by "unexpected" bills that come round every year
- you've tried tracking your spending and found it told you what went wrong but didn't stop it happening

## Does zero-based budgeting work with irregular income?

It does, with one change: budget from your lowest typical month, not your average month. Assign that amount in full. When a better month comes in, give the extra pounds jobs straight away, starting with the emergency fund and the next month's bills. Over time this helps you live on last month's income instead of this month's, which takes most of the pressure out of payday.
