# Pay Yourself First: How to Set It Up in the UK (and Why It Works)

> **Quick answer:** "Pay yourself first" means your savings are the first
> bill you pay each month. A standing order moves a fixed amount out of your
> current account on the day after payday, before any other spending, and
> you live on what's left. It works because it relies on automation, not
> willpower. Start with whatever you can manage (even 1-5% of take-home
> pay) and raise it each time your pay goes up, working towards 10-20%.

It's one of the oldest ideas in personal finance. *The Richest Man in Babylon* put it as "a part of all you earn is yours to keep" nearly a hundred years ago. (See [The Richest Man in Babylon, with modern UK tools](/blog/richest-man-in-babylon-modern-guide/).) What's changed is how easy it now is to automate.

## Why does paying yourself first work?

Most people save what's left at the end of the month. But spending tends to grow to fill whatever is in the account, so there's rarely anything left. Paying yourself first reverses the order:

- **The decision is made once**, when you set up the standing order, not every month when you're tempted.
- **You adjust your spending to the smaller balance** without much effort, the same way you would to a pay cut you never noticed.
- **It treats your future self like any other creditor:** paid on time, every month.

## How do you set up pay yourself first in the UK?

1. **Open a separate savings account**, ideally at a different bank, so the money isn't sitting in your banking app every time you check your balance.
2. **Set up a standing order** from your current account for the day after payday. Not the end of the month.
3. **Pick an amount you won't cancel.** A small amount you keep paying beats a big amount you stop in March.
4. **Increase it on every pay rise.** Send half of each rise to the standing order before you get used to spending it. That's the best protection against [lifestyle creep](/blog/lifestyle-creep/).
5. **Use more than one pot if it helps:** emergency fund first, then sinking funds, then long-term investing.

Your workplace pension already pays you first, because contributions come out before your pay arrives. See [Workplace Pension Auto-Enrolment](/blog/workplace-pension-auto-enrolment/).

## How much should you pay yourself first?

Start with what you can manage and build up towards 10%, then 20%. If you have a specific goal, work the figure out with the method in [How Much Should I Save Each Month?](/blog/how-much-to-save-each-month/). If you don't have an emergency fund yet, that's where the first payments go. See [How Much Emergency Fund Do You Actually Need?](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund/).

### Worked Example

- Take-home pay: £2,400/month
- Paid first: 10%, £240/month by standing order the day after payday
- Savings rate: 4% a year, compounded monthly
- Period: 5 years
→ Paid in: £14,400 · Balance after 5 years: £15,912

That's a full emergency fund for most households, or a solid start on a house deposit, built from a standing order you set up once.

## What if paying yourself first leaves you short?

Then the amount is too high for now, or the rest of the budget needs work. Lower the standing order rather than cancelling it, so the habit survives, and use the [Budget Planner](/tools/budget/) to find where the squeeze is. If you're carrying expensive debt, a small pay-yourself-first amount for a starter emergency fund plus maximum debt payments is usually the right balance.

*Worked figures use monthly compounding at a constant rate. Savings rates change, and interest outside an ISA may be taxable above your Personal Savings Allowance. This is not financial advice.*
