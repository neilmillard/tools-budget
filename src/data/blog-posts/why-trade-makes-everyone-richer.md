# Why Trade Isn't a Zero-Sum Game (And What It Means for Your Time)

> **Quick answer:** Trade isn't a contest with a winner and a loser —
> both sides only agree to it because it leaves them better off, or they
> wouldn't do it. This holds even when one side is better at *everything*:
> a lawyer earning £300/hour who's also great at cleaning should still
> hire a cleaner at £20/hour and spend that hour practising law, because
> the £280 gained beats the cost of doing both jobs personally. The same
> logic applies to any hour you spend on a low-value task you could
> delegate.

Trade gets described in political language as a competition — countries "winning" or "losing" to each other. That framing doesn't survive contact with a simple question: if trade only benefited one side, why would the other side keep doing it, year after year, for centuries?

## Does trading make both sides richer, or just one?

Take the simplest possible trade: you have a sandwich, a friend has pizza, and you both want a bit of the other's lunch. You swap halves. The total amount of food in the world hasn't changed — but you're both happier than before, because you both now have a mix you each prefer. Nobody "won." Both of you did.

Scale that up to countries, and the mechanism is identical. A country buys something from another because it's cheaper than making it domestically; the seller sells because they get paid what they want for it. Both sides end up with more total value than if each had tried to make everything themselves.

## How can trade still help even when one side is better at everything?

This is the part that surprises most people. Imagine a lawyer who charges £300 an hour and happens to also be an excellent cleaner — genuinely faster and better at it than the cleaner they could hire. Should they clean their own office to save the £20/hour cleaning fee?

The arithmetic says no:

- **Clean it yourself:** save £20, but lose an hour that could have earned £300 in legal work — net position: –£280 relative to the alternative.
- **Hire a cleaner:** pay £20, keep the hour for legal work worth £300 — net position: +£280.

Even being *better* at cleaning doesn't change the answer, because the real cost of cleaning it yourself isn't £20 — it's the £300 you didn't earn instead. This is comparative advantage: what matters isn't who's better at a task in isolation, it's the relative cost of each option, given everything else you could be doing with that time.

## Where does this show up in an ordinary week?

Most people run a version of the lawyer's mistake every week without noticing: doing a low-value task personally because delegating it "costs money," while ignoring what that hour is worth spent elsewhere.

- **Admin, cleaning, or errands** you could pay someone else to do, while your own time is worth more spent on higher-value work — or genuine rest that sustains that work.
- **DIY financial admin** that eats hours better spent once — versus using a calculator built for the job, like the [Budget Planner](/tools/budget/), to get the same answer in minutes.
- **A skill you're mediocre at but keep doing yourself,** because paying someone competent feels like an avoidable expense rather than a trade that frees up higher-value time.

The question worth asking isn't "can I do this myself?" — almost everyone can do almost everything themselves, badly, given enough time. It's "does doing this myself cost more than what my time is actually worth right now?"

*The sandwich/pizza and lawyer/cleaner illustrations of comparative advantage follow the treatment of trade in Thomas Sowell's* Basic Economics*, used to explain the mechanics of trade generally, not as commentary on any specific trade policy.*
