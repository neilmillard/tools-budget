# Your Salary Is a Price, Too: Getting Paid for Value, Not Time

> **Quick answer:** Pay is a price like any other — set roughly by the
> value someone's work creates, not by how hard or how long they work.
> A lemonade stand can profitably pay a fast, effective helper up to the
> full extra revenue they generate, but paying a slow, ineffective one
> the same rate loses money every hour. The practical takeaway: your
> income grows fastest when you increase the *value* your work creates,
> not just the hours you put in.

It feels intuitive that harder physical work should pay more, and gentler work should pay less. In practice, pay tracks something else almost entirely: the value created, as judged by what someone else is willing to pay for it.

## Why doesn't the hardest job always pay the most?

Construction work is physically demanding; nursing carries life-and-death responsibility. Neither is reliably among the highest-paid professions, which confuses a lot of people who assume pay should track effort or importance. It doesn't — it tracks the value created, at the margin, for whoever is paying.

A simple worked example makes this concrete. Imagine running a lemonade stand and considering two helpers:

- **Maria** is fast and friendly. With her help, you sell 10 extra cups a day at £1 each — £10 of extra value.
- **Jake** is slow and distracted. With his help, you sell only 2 extra cups a day — £2 of extra value.

You can profitably pay Maria up to £10 a day and still break even. Paying Jake more than £2 a day loses you money every single day he works. This isn't a judgement of either person's worth — it's simple arithmetic about the value each one adds.

## What happens when pay is set above the value being created?

This is also why a minimum wage set above a worker's marginal value doesn't simply mean "the same job, better pay" — it changes the underlying arithmetic for the employer. If a role reliably creates £10/hour of value and the mandated wage is £15/hour, the employer loses £5 every hour that role is filled. The typical responses aren't malicious, they're mechanical: automate the role, reduce hours, or stop hiring for it. The intention (higher pay) and the incentive it creates (fewer of those jobs) point in opposite directions — the same pattern as [rewarding intentions vs. incentives](/blog/rewards-not-intentions/) generally.

## How do you actually earn more, given this?

If pay tracks value created rather than hours worked or effort expended, the highest-leverage lever for growing your income is straightforward, if not easy: increase the value your work creates, specifically the value that's scarce and hard to replace.

- **Learn the skill fewer people have.** Scarcity is doing real work in every wage number — common skills command common pay, regardless of how hard they are to perform.
- **Make your value legible.** Value that isn't visible to whoever pays you can't be priced fairly. Track outcomes, not hours, wherever you can.
- **Model what a raise actually changes.** Before negotiating or changing roles, use the [Budget Planner](/tools/budget/) to see what a given pay change genuinely does to your monthly position, not just your headline salary.

Your salary isn't a reward for showing up. It's a price — and like any price, it moves with value, not effort alone.

*The lemonade-stand wage example and minimum-wage mechanics referenced here follow the account in Thomas Sowell's* Basic Economics*, used to illustrate how wages function as prices generally, not as a position on minimum-wage policy.*
