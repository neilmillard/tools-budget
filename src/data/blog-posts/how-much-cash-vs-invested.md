# How Much of Your Money Should Be in Cash vs Invested?

> **Quick answer:** Keep 3-6 months of essential expenses in cash as an
> emergency fund, plus anything earmarked for a goal within the next
> 5 years. Everything beyond that — money you genuinely won't need for
> 5+ years — can reasonably be invested, since cash reliably loses
> purchasing power to inflation over long periods while diversified
> investments have historically outpaced it. On £30,000 total savings
> with £9,000 needed as an emergency fund, that leaves £21,000 eligible
> for investing if no shorter-term goal needs it.

The question isn't "cash or invest" as a single decision — it's sorting each pound by when you'll actually need it, then matching the account to that timeline.

## How do you work out your own cash-vs-invest split?

- Monthly essential expenses: £1,500
- Target emergency fund (6 months): £9,000
- Total current savings: £30,000
- Known short-term goal (holiday, car, within 2 years): none in this example
→ Cash needed: £9,000 (emergency fund only, since no other short-term goal exists)
→ Remaining eligible for investing: £30,000 − £9,000 = £21,000

Any known expense due within roughly 5 years — a wedding, a car replacement, a house deposit — should be added to the cash side of this calculation before the remainder is considered "investable."

## Why does holding too much in cash actually cost you money?

Cash held beyond your emergency fund and short-term goals loses real value to inflation every year it sits uninvested — at 3% average inflation, £21,000 held in cash for 10 years retains only the purchasing power of roughly £15,600 in today's terms, even though the number on the statement hasn't fallen. Investing that same amount in a diversified portfolio has historically outpaced inflation over long periods, though with short-term volatility cash doesn't have.

## Does your age or risk tolerance change the split?

Time horizon matters more than age directly, but age often correlates with it — someone decades from retirement can generally afford a higher proportion of invested money relative to cash than someone a few years from needing to draw on it, since there's more time to recover from a market downturn. Risk tolerance is personal, though: two people with identical timelines can reasonably choose different splits based on how much short-term volatility they can tolerate without making a panicked decision.

## What's the actual first step?

Confirm your emergency fund is fully funded and any near-term goal is covered in cash before directing new money toward investing — the split only works in order, not simultaneously from a standing start.
