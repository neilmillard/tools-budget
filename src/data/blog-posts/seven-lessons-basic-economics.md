# Seven Economics Lessons That Explain Why Things Cost What They Cost

> **Quick answer:** Most everyday financial confusion — why a "helpful"
> price cap creates shortages, why a pay rise doesn't guarantee a job
> stays, why a cheaper car can be the more expensive choice — traces back
> to seven basic economic principles: incentives beat intentions,
> every choice has a cost, prices carry information, price controls
> distort supply, profit and loss are feedback, wages are a price too,
> and trade benefits both sides. Understanding these seven doesn't
> require an economics degree, and it makes a lot of "surprising"
> financial news stop being surprising.

Economics has a reputation for being complicated, full of jargon and graphs. Most of what actually matters for a normal financial life comes down to a handful of ideas, each simple enough to explain with a lemonade stand or a school lunch line. Here's the full set, with a link to the deeper dive on each.

## 1. People respond to rewards, not intentions

When Maryland raised taxes on its highest earners in 2008 expecting $106 million in new revenue, roughly a quarter of the state's millionaires left within a year — and the state lost $257 million instead. Intentions don't move money or behaviour. Incentives do. → [Rewards, Not Promises](/blog/rewards-not-intentions/)

## 2. Every choice has a cost, seen or unseen

A £50,000 car isn't a £50,000 decision if a £20,000 equivalent existed — it's a £50,000 decision *plus* whatever the extra £30,000 could have grown into if invested instead. The real cost of anything is what you gave up to get it, not just its price tag. → [The Price You Don't See](/blog/every-choice-has-a-cost/)

## 3. Prices carry information nobody else can gather

Nobody plans how much fresh fish reaches London each morning, or how many pizza toppings a city needs this week. Prices coordinate it, continuously, by signalling scarcity and abundance in real time — a job no person or office could do manually. → [Nobody's in Charge of Pizza](/blog/prices-are-messages/)

## 4. Interfering with a price doesn't remove a shortage — it relocates it

Cap a price too low, and supply dries up (empty shelves, abandoned rental buildings). Guarantee a price too high, and unsold surplus piles up instead (rotting warehouses of wheat). Either direction, the shortage or surplus doesn't disappear — it just moves somewhere less visible. → [Why Cheaper Never Stays Cheap](/blog/why-price-controls-backfire/)

## 5. Profit says "do more," loss says "stop" — and both are honest

A loss isn't a moral failure, it's information: this isn't working, redirect the resources. The feedback loop only breaks when losses get propped up artificially instead of acted on — whether that's a "too big to fail" bailout or a personal side project kept alive on sunk-cost loyalty. → [Profit and Loss: The Only Honest Feedback](/blog/profit-and-loss-as-feedback/)

## 6. Your wage is a price, set by value created, not effort spent

Pay tracks the value work creates at the margin, not how hard or how long someone works — which is why a fast, effective helper can profitably be paid far more than a slow one doing the same job. Growing your income means growing the value you create, not just the hours you log. → [Your Salary Is a Price, Too](/blog/your-wage-is-a-price-too/)

## 7. Trade benefits both sides, even when one side is "better at everything"

A lawyer earning £300/hour should still hire a £20/hour cleaner, even if they'd personally clean faster — because the hour spent cleaning costs £300 in lost legal work, not £20. The same logic applies to any low-value task worth delegating in your own week. → [Why Trade Isn't a Zero-Sum Game](/blog/why-trade-makes-everyone-richer/)

## Why do these seven ideas keep showing up in ordinary financial decisions?

Because they're not really seven separate rules — they're one underlying idea, viewed from seven angles: resources are limited, so every decision about them (by a government, a company, or a household) has a real cost and a real consequence, whether or not that cost is visible at the moment of the decision. Prices, wages, profits, and losses are just the different names that cost takes depending on where you're standing.

None of these seven require memorising formulas. They require the habit of asking, every time something looks free, cheap, or unfair: what's the real trade-off here, and what does this price or pay figure actually being telling me?
