# How to Save Money on Groceries in the UK (Without Eating Worse)

> **Quick answer:** The biggest grocery savings come from deciding your
> weekly food budget before you shop, planning meals around it, and buying
> only what's on the list. After that: compare the price per kg or per litre
> rather than the pack price, drop one brand level (branded to own-brand,
> premium to standard), use loyalty-card prices, and cut the food you throw
> away. Together these typically take 10-20% off a household's food bill
> without changing what you actually eat.

Food is one of the few big household costs you can change this week. Rent, energy tariffs and council tax mostly can't be changed quickly, but the weekly shop can. That makes it one of the best places to free up money for the Debt and Saving stages.

## How much should you spend on groceries?

There's no right number. Your number should be one you've **chosen**, not one you notice afterwards on your bank statement. Look back at four weeks of food spending (supermarket, top-up shops, and work lunches if you buy them), take the weekly average, and set a target a little below it. Give that money a fixed place in your budget, as we describe in [Zero-Based Budgeting](/blog/zero-based-budgeting/), and the target becomes real.

## What are the most effective ways to cut your food shop?

1. **Plan meals for the week before you shop**, starting with what's already in the fridge and cupboards.
2. **Shop from a list, once a week.** Every extra "just popping in" trip adds things that weren't on the list.
3. **Compare unit prices.** UK supermarkets show the price per kg, litre or 100g on the shelf label. The bigger pack isn't always cheaper.
4. **Drop one brand level.** Own-brand staples (pasta, rice, tinned tomatoes, cleaning products) are often hard to tell apart from big brands.
5. **Use loyalty-card prices** if you shop there anyway, but don't buy something just because it's on offer.
6. **Cook once, eat twice.** Batch-cook and freeze portions so a busy evening doesn't turn into a takeaway.
7. **Waste less.** Use the freezer, eat the oldest food first, and shop for meals rather than "ingredients that might be useful".
8. **Try a discount supermarket** for your basics, even if you still go elsewhere for a few favourites.
9. **Shop online if you're prone to impulse buys.** The basket total is in front of you the whole time, and so is the delete button.

## How much can you realistically save?

### Worked Example

- Current weekly food spend: £110
- Changes: meal plan + list, own-brand staples, one fewer top-up shop a week
- New weekly food spend: £95
→ Saved: £15/week · £65/month · £780/year

£65 a month is enough to start an emergency fund, add an extra payment on a credit card, or feed a sinking fund for next Christmas.

## How do you keep Christmas food costs under control?

Christmas week is the most expensive food shop of the year, and most of the extra goes on things bought "just in case".

- **Plan the actual meals**, from Christmas Eve to Boxing Day, and buy for those meals, not for a fortnight.
- **Set a separate Christmas food budget**, ideally from a [sinking fund](/blog/sinking-funds-envelope-method/) you filled through the year.
- **Buy long-life items early** and fresh items last.
- **Use leftovers on purpose.** Plan a Boxing Day meal around the leftovers instead of doing another shop.

## What should you do with the money you save?

Move it on the day you make the saving. If the weekly shop comes in £15 under budget, send £15 to savings or debt that evening. Money left in a current account has a habit of being spent on something else, which we look at in [Lifestyle Creep](/blog/lifestyle-creep/).
